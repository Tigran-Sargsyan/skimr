## Key Points

- Archaeology shows a rapid rise in symbolic art and tool innovation between 100,000 and 50,000 years ago.
- A popular hypothesis posits a key genetic change, such as for complex language, driving this cultural shift.
- Reich’s team scanned genomes for recent selective sweeps shared by all humans in the last 100,000–200,000 years.
- They found no loci where all modern humans share a common ancestor more recent than about 400,000–500,000 years ago.
- This implies no single, universal mutation explains the cultural changes seen 100,000–50,000 years ago.
- Most living humans descend from a small ancestral group of roughly 10,000 individuals from 100,000–50,000 years ago.
- Highly divergent African groups like Khoisan and rainforest hunter-gatherers still share full modern cognitive capacities.
- Polygenic adaptation and environment-specific selection on existing variation likely drove recent trait changes, including in the last 18,000 years for many complex traits studied.

## Notes

## Cultural “quickening” and the single-mutation hypothesis

Between roughly 100,000 and 50,000 years ago, the archaeological record shows a marked acceleration in cultural change.
- There is the first extensive representational art, including drawings on walls and bead necklaces.
- Tool technology begins innovating more rapidly, with new forms and increasing complexity.

This pattern led to a hypothesis: perhaps a key genetic change occurred in humans during this window.
- The imagined mutation might underlie complex or representational language or other cognitive capacities.
- The idea is that a beneficial mutation arose, swept rapidly through the population, and enabled symbolic culture.

## Searching for a universal recent selective sweep

Reich and colleagues explicitly tested this hypothesis in a 2016 study led by Shamil Malik and others.
- They scanned human DNA for regions where all, or nearly all, living humans share a very recent common ancestor.
- They specifically looked for signals suggesting a classic “hard sweep” in the last ~100,000–200,000 years.

Their result: they did not find any such regions younger than about 400,000–500,000 years.
- Across the genome, no loci showed a common ancestor for all present-day humans that recent.
- This absence is surprising if a single key mutation had driven the cultural changes around 100,000–50,000 years ago.

Thus, there is no genomic evidence for a universal, recent selective sweep that is shared by everyone today and maps neatly onto the archaeological “revolution.”

## Implications: no single biological “on–off switch”

This result suggests that the striking cultural changes did not come from one decisive mutation.
- There appears to be no “key selective sweep” in that period that is ancestral to all modern humans.
- Earlier in the conversation, they had discussed no such sweeps distinguishing Europeans from East Asians; here, the claim is even broader, covering all humans.

Reich allows that biological adaptation might still have occurred during this era.
- Instead of one big change, adaptation could be polygenic: many mutations with small effects shifting in the same direction.
- Such polygenic shifts could move populations to new phenotypic “set points” without any single mutation rising to near-universal frequency.

## Shared ancestry and present-day cognitive capacity

The population that lived about 100,000–50,000 years ago is ancestrally important.
- It contributed ancestry to all non-Africans, most East Africans, and West Africans.
- Estimates suggest this ancestral group was small, on the order of about 10,000 individuals.

Some present-day African groups have additional ancestry from more divergent lineages.
- Khoisan groups of southern Africa and Central African rainforest hunter-gatherers derive substantial ancestry from lineages that diverged ~200,000 years ago from other humans.

Despite these deep splits, all these groups today share comparable cognitive abilities.
- Members of all these populations can, for example, attend college and perform the same intellectual tasks.
- There is no evidence of any key cognitive mutation present in some groups and absent in others.

This supports the idea that modern human cognitive potential was already present in the shared ancestral population, rather than being introduced by a single later mutation.

## Latent variation and the “clay” for traits

Reich and others in human genetics conceive of human populations as containing extensive standing variation.
- The small ancestral population already carried much of the genetic “clay” from which many traits can be shaped.
- Differences across groups today can arise from different ways this existing variation is combined and favored.

Environmental and selection conditions influence how mean trait values shift.
- When conditions change, selection can alter the distribution of allele frequencies across many loci.
- This can produce substantial phenotypic change without requiring new unique mutations.

## Evidence for recent polygenic shifts

Reich mentions ongoing work examining selection on complex traits in the more recent past.
- They studied more than 500 traits in this part of the world over the last 18,000, 10,000, and 5,000 years.
- About 100 complex traits show significant directional movement in this timeframe.

This suggests that human populations have responded measurably to their environments in relatively recent millennia.
- Selection has shifted trait distributions systematically for at least a subset of important complex traits.
- These changes may even be stronger in the last 18,000 years than in earlier periods, at least in the region studied.

Overall, the evidence points toward many small, environment-contingent genetic shifts over time, rather than a single transformative mutation that suddenly made humans creative.
