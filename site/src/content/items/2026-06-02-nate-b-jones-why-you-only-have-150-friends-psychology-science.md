## Key Points

- Robin Dunbar’s 1992 research ties primate neocortex size to limits on relationship complexity.
- Humans typically maintain about five people in a core, most intimate relationship group.
- People tend to have around 15 relationships characterized by deep trust.
- Humans can sustain about 50 meaningful working relationships at a time.
- Humans generally cap out at roughly 150 stable social connections overall.
- Army mathematicians empirically observed communication effectiveness peaks at group sizes of 5, 15, 50, and 150.
- Military organizations study these group-size limits because communication failures have especially high stakes for them.

## Notes

## Dunbar’s Research and Relationship Limits

Robin Dunbar’s work in 1992 connected primate brain structure, specifically neocortex size, with how many relationships a species can handle. From this research emerged the idea that humans have layered limits on the complexity and number of social relationships they can maintain. These limits are not a new discovery but have been established since the early 1990s. The structure is hierarchical, with several distinct tiers rather than one single cap.

## The Layered Social Circles

The innermost tier is a group of about five people, who form an individual’s core circle. These are the closest relationships, involving strong emotional bonds and high levels of interaction. The next layer expands to about 15 people marked by deep trust, where individuals still maintain strong, reliable connections. Beyond that, there is a group of roughly 50 people with whom a person can sustain meaningful working relationships, allowing for coordinated activity and collaboration. The broadest stable layer is around 150 people, representing the maximum number of stable social connections that can be actively maintained.

## Empirical Confirmation by the Military

Army mathematicians tested and confirmed these group-size patterns in practical settings. When they examined communication effectiveness inside groups, they found that small teams of about five people functioned most efficiently. Communication performance showed notable peaks again at group sizes of 15, 50, and 150. These findings aligned with the relationship tiers suggested by Dunbar’s research. The military investigates these patterns thoroughly because the consequences of poor communication can be extremely serious in their context, so they need to know what group structures work best.
