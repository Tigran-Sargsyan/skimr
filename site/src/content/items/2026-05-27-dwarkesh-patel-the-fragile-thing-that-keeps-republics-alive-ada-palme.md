## Key Points

- Many medieval and Renaissance Italian city-states lived in constant fear of monarchic takeover.
- Over centuries, numerous republics were gradually worn away, leaving only a few strong survivors.
- Transitions between monarchy and republic could alternate rapidly when hated dynasties were overthrown.
- Milan briefly restored a republic after the Visconti duke’s death created a power vacuum.
- Once free, Milan’s Guelph and Ghibelline factions prioritized persecuting each other over preserving the new republic.
- A threatened faction invited the exiled ducal heir back, trading restoration of monarchy for personal safety.
- Machiavelli concludes republican institutions are only as strong as citizens’ attachment to and defense of them.
- When people value factional victory, wealth, or bribes more than liberty, republics collapse; when they prioritize liberty, republics endure.

## Notes

## The Long Survival and Attrition of Italian Republics

- Italian city-state republics existed for centuries after the fall of the Roman Empire while constantly fearing monarchic takeover.
- At first, there were many republics, but over time they disappeared one by one, like rocks eroding on a beach.
- The continued existence of a few enduring republics can be misleading, because it hides the larger number that once existed and have since vanished.

## Monarchy–Republic Alternation

- Political evolution is not strictly one-way from republic to monarchy; some states oscillate between the two forms.
- When a ruling family becomes widely hated, rebellions can topple the monarchy and sometimes establish a new republic.
- These new republics are often fragile and can be overthrown in turn, leading to another monarchy, creating rapid alternation between systems.

## Machiavelli’s Focus: Civic Commitment to Republicanism

- Machiavelli argues that the key variable is how deeply the city’s population cares about being a republic.
- A republic’s survival depends on citizens being emotionally and politically invested in maintaining republican liberty.
- Without a strong, shared attachment to the republican form, institutional safeguards are not enough to prevent collapse.

## Case Study: Milan’s Brief Republic

- Milan had historically been a republic but later fell under rule by the Visconti ducal family.
- The last Visconti duke died leaving an ambiguous will, splitting claims between his illegitimate daughter and the king of Naples, creating a temporary power vacuum.
- In that moment of uncertainty, the people of Milan rose up and declared a new republic.
- They established new republican institutions, including a city council, suggesting an attempt at genuine institutional reconstruction.

## Ancient Factions Resurface: Guelphs vs. Ghibellines

- Milan had long-standing political factions, the Guelphs and the Ghibellines, whose rivalry stretched back centuries.
- Under monarchical rule, these factions had been constrained and unable to openly persecute one another for roughly a century.
- Once the republic was restored, old hatreds reemerged immediately, as the factions were newly free to act on their animosities.
- The faction in power began massacring and persecuting the opposing faction, prioritizing vengeance and dominance over stability.

## Self-Destruction of the Republic

- Facing massacre, the persecuted faction sought survival by appealing to the exiled son-in-law of the deceased duke.
- They invited him back to restore monarchical rule in exchange for protection from their enemies.
- Both factions demonstrated, in Machiavelli’s reading, that they cared more about defeating each other than preserving the republic.
- Their willingness to sacrifice republican liberty for factional advantage directly facilitated the republic’s collapse and the return of monarchy.

## Machiavelli’s General Lesson on Republican Fragility

- Machiavelli uses this example to illustrate that republican institutions depend fundamentally on citizens’ priorities and values.
- If citizens place higher importance on factional victory, personal wealth, or other incentives than on liberty, the republic is doomed.
- He notes that people can be bribed or seduced by wealth and corrupt offers of power, weakening their commitment to shared institutions.
- Conversely, when a population is deeply invested in defending the liberty of its republic, that republic can endure much longer.
- Machiavelli suggests that Milan’s republic might, with genuine solidarity and patriotism, have survived for decades, which he treats as a meaningful achievement even if eventual foreign conquest was likely.

## The Fragile Core of Republican Durability

- The story underlines a central claim: republican survival is not guaranteed by structure alone but by collective will.
- Institutional balances, councils, and laws work only when backed by citizens who prefer preserving the republic over the temptations of factional gain or material reward.
- The “fragile thing” that keeps republics alive is the people’s active, prioritized commitment to republican liberty above rival loyalties and incentives.
