## Key Points

- Rejections and corrections in computer work are knowledge creation events, not null actions.
- Most people fail to capture the knowledge created when they reject or correct something.
- These rejection moments encode constraints, rules, and personal or organizational taste.
- If captured, this encoded knowledge can compound and improve over time in AI workflows.
- Currently, this feedback usually evaporates into transient channels like email, chat, and Slack.
- The speaker sees almost nobody asking how to systematically capture and scale these “no” moments.
- He argues this lost feedback is a major, expensive mistake in how people use AI.
- The proposed opportunity is to turn moments of rejection into reusable, scalable assets in AI systems.

## Notes

## Rejection as Knowledge Creation

The speaker frames a common pattern in computer-based work: we constantly reject, correct, or say “no” to outputs, whether from humans, tools, or AI systems. He emphasizes that these rejections are not empty or null actions. Each “no” is a moment where new knowledge is created, because it clarifies what is not acceptable and what is preferred instead.

He argues that in these brief interactions, we implicitly define constraints, rules, and expressions of taste. When you say, “Not like this, but like that,” you are encoding standards, style, and domain-specific judgment. These judgments are exactly the kind of information that could make AI workflows smarter and more aligned over time.

## The Lost Value of Negative Feedback

Despite their importance, these knowledge-creation events are almost never captured in a structured, reusable way. The speaker notes that they typically end up buried in ephemeral channels: an email reply, a chat message, a comment thread, or a Slack conversation.

Because they live in such transient places, this feedback effectively evaporates once the immediate task is done. No one is systematically storing it, indexing it, or turning it into explicit rules or guidelines. As a result, the same kinds of mistakes recur, and the same corrections must be made repeatedly.

## Compounding Constraints and Taste

The speaker highlights that the constraints and encoded taste contained in these rejection moments have the potential to compound. If each “no” were captured and integrated into an AI workflow, the system could progressively internalize more of an organization’s preferences and rules.

Over time, this compounding process would allow AI tools to produce outputs that require fewer corrections, because prior “no” moments would have been learned from and embedded. The value of each correction would thus extend beyond the single interaction in which it occurred.

## The Missed Opportunity in Current AI Workflows

He stresses that almost nobody is asking the key question: how do we capture these “no” moments and scale them? The current dominant practice treats them as transient, one-off interactions rather than as data points.

In his view, this oversight is a major, expensive mistake in how people are using AI. By not deliberately collecting and systematizing rejections and corrections, individuals and organizations forgo the chance to turn everyday feedback into a growing, reusable asset that continually improves their AI-supported work.
