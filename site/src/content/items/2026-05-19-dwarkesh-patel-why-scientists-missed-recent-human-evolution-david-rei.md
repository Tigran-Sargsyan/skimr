## Key Points

- Ancient DNA has so far been more successful at reconstructing human migrations than biological change.
- Studying biological adaptation over time requires large ancient sample sizes to track allele frequency shifts precisely.
- Allele frequency changes that are too extreme to be due to chance signal natural selection in response to environmental change.
- Past consensus held that directional natural selection in humans has been minimal over the last several hundred thousand years.
- Comparisons between Europeans and East Asians show surprisingly few alleles that are near-fixation differences, reinforcing this earlier view.
- Reich’s team partitions frequency changes and finds about 98% are explained by drift and population structure, not directional selection.
- Large-scale migrations and population replacements produce huge genome-wide shifts that obscure locus-specific selection signals.
- By examining many small, relatively isolated populations between major migrations, consistent directional shifts in the same allele across groups reveal pervasive recent natural selection in humans.

## Notes

## Original Dream of Ancient DNA and Why It Stalled on Biology

- At the start of ancient DNA research ~16–17 years ago, a central hope was to trace biological change through time by sequencing ancient human remains.
- That biological dream largely went unrealized for years, even as the field became highly successful at reconstructing population history.
- Ancient genomes showed that present-day people often are not descended from earlier inhabitants of the same region, that mixture is common, and that sex-biased processes are frequent, overturning many archaeological assumptions.
- However, learning how particular functional variants (e.g., for skin pigmentation, lactase persistence, behavior) changed in frequency over time requires something history-focused work did not: very large sample sizes.
- A single genome is rich for history because it encodes ancestry from thousands to hundreds of thousands of ancestors, allowing precise placement relative to other genomes.
- For allele frequency trajectories, though, each individual provides essentially one or two draws of each variant, so robust inference needs many individuals per time slice.
- Only in the last few years has the field amassed enough ancient individuals with good-quality data to attempt systematic study of biological adaptation over time.

## Why Frequency Changes Matter for Biology

- The aim is to use “experiments of nature” over tens of thousands of years to identify which genetic variants are biologically significant.
- Environmental shifts—adoption of agriculture, close living with domesticated animals, moving to different climates or altitudes—create new selective pressures.
- Adaptive alleles (e.g., aiding high-altitude survival, altered behavior helpful in new social or ecological settings) should systematically increase in frequency in populations facing those conditions.
- Detecting modest shifts—on the order of a few or 10 percentage points—requires very large sample sizes; otherwise, such changes are indistinguishable from random noise.
- The statistical task is to distinguish frequency changes too extreme to be explained by chance alone, thereby highlighting loci under selection.

## Earlier Consensus: Recent Human Selection Seen as Quiescent

- For several decades, mainstream thinking held that natural selection on humans had been largely quiet over the last several hundred thousand years.
- One key argument came from comparing allele frequencies between continental groups such as Europeans and East Asians.
- These groups share a common ancestral population that left Africa and the Middle East roughly 40–50,000 years ago—about 1,500–2,000 generations, enough time for strongly beneficial alleles to potentially reach fixation.
- Yet when geneticists looked for mutations with very large frequency differences between Europeans and East Asians, they found almost no alleles that are close to 100% different in frequency.
- This pattern suggested that strong, directional selection driving dramatic divergence since the split had been rare; most changes looked consistent with random drift and modest selection.
- Under this view, humans might have reached some adaptive “optimum” hundreds of thousands of years ago, with recent selection mostly purging deleterious mutations rather than pushing to new adaptive peaks.

## Partitioning Frequency Change: Drift, Structure, and Selection

- Reich’s group leveraged ~10 million variable genomic positions to quantify how much observed allele frequency change is due to directional selection versus other processes.
- They explicitly modeled contributions from genetic drift and population structure (including migration and mixture) as distinct from adaptive directional selection.
- Their estimate is that about 98% of the total variance in allele frequency changes is explained by non-adaptive processes, especially drift and demographic history.
- This means adaptive signals are a small minority of total change and hence very hard to detect against the noisy background of demographic events.
- Despite being only a small proportion of the variance, their analysis indicates that directional selection has nonetheless been widespread across the genome once it is carefully teased apart from drift and structure.

## Why Migration and Replacement Are Statistically Unhelpful for Locus-Specific Selection

- The interviewer raises the idea that large-scale population replacement might itself be a form of selection at a group level.
- Reich acknowledges this possibility: a replacing population may succeed due to cultural advantages (technology, practices) and may also carry genetic traits that contribute to their success.
- However, in the data, a replacement shows up as a genome-wide shift where nearly all allele frequencies change together because ancestry changes wholesale.
- For detecting locus-specific natural selection, the critical question is whether a particular mutation changes more than the rest of the genome would predict under neutral demographic processes.
- Massive migrations create huge frequency shifts that are poorly informative for this purpose, because they affect almost all loci similarly and swamp subtle selection effects.
- Statistically, these episodes become “bad moments” for identifying selection at individual sites.

## Using Many Small, Stable Populations as Natural Experiments

- The study treats the history of Europe and the Middle East as an “archipelago” of small, relatively isolated populations separated in space and short time intervals.
- Between major migration and mixture events, there are stretches of a few hundred years where local populations in regions like Britain or Hungary are fairly stable.
- Each such interval and location serves as a small natural experiment in which selection can act on standing variation.
- By examining whether a given allele slightly increases in frequency within many such independent populations, researchers can look for consistent directional trends.
- If across multiple populations and time windows the same allele repeatedly drifts upward in frequency while demographic noise averages out, this pattern indicates directional selection.
- Contrast this with periods like ~4,500 years ago in Europe, when steppe pastoralists (Yamnaya-related groups) migrated in and contributed ~40–80% of the ancestry, causing enormous shifts in nearly all allele frequencies.
- Those large shifts primarily reflect ancestry change, not necessarily selection on specific sites, and thus are down-weighted or treated cautiously when searching for selection.
- By assembling data across many “quiet” intervals and locations, the study can isolate selection signals that are otherwise hidden within the much larger background of drift and migration-driven change.

## Overall Implication from the Preprint

- Once ancient sample sizes became large enough and an appropriate framework for separating demography from selection was applied, evidence emerged that directional natural selection has been common in recent human history.
- The earlier impression of quiescent recent evolution was largely an artifact of limited data and reliance on present-day population comparisons, which are dominated by drift and structure.
- Ancient DNA, analyzed at scale and across many small, relatively stable population intervals, now reveals that recent human evolution has involved substantial, genome-wide adaptation even though its signal is subtle compared to demographic noise.
