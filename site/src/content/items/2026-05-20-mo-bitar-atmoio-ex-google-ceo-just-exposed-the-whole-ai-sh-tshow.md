## Key Points

- A prominent OpenAI engineer reportedly spent $1.3 million on tokens in 30 days as an experiment.
- The experiment asked how software would be built if token costs were effectively irrelevant.
- Low-end model prices have fallen or stayed flat while high-end model prices have been rising.
- From GPT 5.4 to 5.5, token prices are claimed to have roughly doubled.
- Anthropic’s new tokenizer makes responses about 35% longer, increasing effective costs without changing listed prices.
- OpenRouter’s analysis suggested GPT 5.5 is roughly 49–92% more expensive than GPT 5.4 in practice.
- Eric Schmidt urged graduates to “say yes to the rocket ship” of AI and was loudly booed.
- The speaker believes AI access advantages will let wealthy users iterate toward product-market fit faster than others.

## Notes

## Token Costs and the “Free AI” Illusion

The video opens with an anecdote about Peter Steinberger, described as the inventor of OpenClaw and an OpenAI employee. He shared a dashboard showing his token spending: about $250,000 over seven days and $1.3 million over thirty days. This spend was framed as a “science experiment” exploring how software would be built if token costs didn’t matter. The creator mocks this premise with analogies like asking how society would look if crack were free or how housing would change if wood were free. These comparisons are used to underscore how detached such experiments are from normal users’ financial reality.

From this, the creator draws a broader point: AI at the cutting edge is not financially accessible to ordinary people and likely never will be at the scale implied by such experiments.

## Pricing Trajectory: Cheap for Some, Expensive Where It Matters

The common belief that AI will get steadily cheaper, following something like Moore’s Law, is labeled a deceptive narrative (though not necessarily intentionally deceptive). The creator distinguishes between two pricing “charts” or tiers. For lower-end models, token prices have been decreasing or at least holding steady. However, for the high-end models needed for “meaningful work,” prices are said to be increasing.

Specific claims include that moving from GPT 5.4 to GPT 5.5 involved a doubling of token prices. For Anthropic’s Opus 4.7, listed prices remained the same as 4.6, but a new tokenizer leads to about 35% longer responses. This effectively raises costs because more tokens are consumed per interaction without changing the per-token price. An OpenRouter study is cited, claiming GPT 5.5 costs 49–92% more than 5.4 in practice.

From these examples, the creator argues that the practical cost of top-tier AI is rising, not falling, contrary to public expectations.

## Concentration of Access and Talent

The video suggests that access to the most powerful models is becoming increasingly restricted. Mythos, a model described as highly desirable, is said to be so locked down that Andrej Karpathy reportedly joined Anthropic just to gain access. The creator humorously comments that even he felt left behind by trying to use more open-access models like Opus 4.7.

This dynamic is extrapolated into a future where Anthropic and OpenAI become the main or only significant employers for top AI talent because of their exclusive access to frontier systems. The implicit claim is that access to capabilities, not just skill, is becoming a key bottleneck.

## Eric Schmidt, the “Rocket Ship,” and Public Backlash

Attention then shifts to Eric Schmidt, former Google CEO, described as having a net worth of $30 billion. He delivered a commencement speech at the University of Arizona where he urged graduates to “say yes to the rocket ship,” defining the rocket ship as AI. Each mention of AI reportedly drew audible boos from the crowd.

Schmidt urged students not to ask “which seat” when invited onto the rocket ship, implying they should simply join. The creator points out the contradiction: the rocket ship metaphorically has only three seats, already occupied by Eric, Sam, and “Daario,” leaving no room for the graduates he is addressing. This is used to illustrate how elites encourage others to join an opportunity that is already monopolized.

The creator jokes that Schmidt slowly realizes his speech is misaligned with the audience’s mood, sensing a “disturbance in the room” as boos mount. They describe this as a potential “bubble pop” moment where the disconnect between AI cheerleading and public sentiment becomes visible.

## The Dilemma for Young People and Builders

The creator asks what an 18-year-old today should learn or practice. Most young people cannot spend hundreds or thousands of dollars per day on tokens. Yet, if their goal is to build products and reach product-market fit (PMF) quickly, AI provides a powerful acceleration tool.

AI allows extremely rapid prototyping: a person can “throw darts” by shipping products quickly and testing market demand before investing extensive manual coding effort. The creator contrasts this with their own first app, which took a full year to build decades ago and ultimately failed because they did not know what they were doing. They now believe they could recreate that app in about half an hour, learning the same lesson much faster.

However, this speed advantage depends on having enough money to spend on tokens. Those with funds can iterate far more quickly toward PMF than those relying on manual effort, creating a structural advantage for the wealthy.

## AI in Companies: Bug Factories and Fast Fixes

Mitchell Hashimoto, founder of HashiCorp and creator of the GoI terminal app, is cited discussing “AI psychosis” in companies. According to the summary presented, many organizations are now optimizing for fixing issues quickly rather than preventing issues in the first place. The underlying claim is that AI is inherently poor at not creating issues; it tends to generate bugs.

At the same time, AI is strong at helping fix those bugs rapidly. This leads to a development culture where speed and post-hoc correction are prioritized over upfront robustness. The creator notes that many companies deploy features based on hypotheses about revenue impact, but often those features fail to move the bottom line.

Given this, companies may prefer shipping slightly more buggy features if they can do so in one-tenth the time, relying on fast iteration and bug fixing afterward. Anthropic is mentioned humorously as having many bugs and being slow to fix them, though the creator admits uncertainty about where they actually fall on the spectrum.

## Adaptation, Optimism, and Limits of Comfort

Airbnb CEO Brian Chesky is quoted saying that as long as someone is smart and willing to adapt and change, they will be fine regardless of where technology goes. The creator finds this perspective sensible: tools that increase productivity and speed might, in theory, create demand for more people who can wield them effectively.

However, the creator refuses to offer a reassuring story about AI’s trajectory. They explicitly state they have not solved the dilemma of AI being “for the rich by the rich” and find the situation depressing. They see no strong incentive for powerful actors to make AI cheaper, given current dynamics.

The final message is pragmatic rather than hopeful: acknowledge that AI is unlikely to become broadly cheap, accept the structural imbalance, and “forward deploy yourself.” The “turtleneck” line is used as a metaphor for adopting a builder’s mindset and inserting oneself where possible into the evolving landscape, even without guarantees of fairness or universal benefit.
