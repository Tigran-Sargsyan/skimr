## Key Points

- Meetings are a coordination mechanism whose value must exceed their coordination cost to be justified.
- In lower-output environments, such as $250,000 per person, many meetings were net positive.
- At higher productivity levels, like $2 million per person, the same meetings often become net negative.
- The value destroyed by unnecessary meetings scales with how productive individual contributors are.
- Discussions about AI and teams tend to focus narrowly on increasing volume of work like code or content.
- An obsession with output volume causes leaders to overlook the rising opportunity cost of coordination time.
- This volume-centric mindset results in organizational decisions that are described as disastrously incorrect.
- The core mistake is ignoring how AI-driven productivity changes the cost-benefit math of meetings and coordination.

## Notes

## Coordination Costs and the Purpose of Meetings

The speaker frames meetings as a tool for coordination, not an inherent good. A meeting only makes sense if the coordination benefit outweighs the cost of pulling people away from productive work. Historically, when per-person output was around $250,000, many meetings could clear that bar because the opportunity cost of an hour of someone’s time was relatively lower.

The key idea is that coordination has a real economic cost, even if it’s rarely quantified. Every participant’s time in a meeting represents forgone output that could have been produced individually. When this forgone output is modest, meetings can frequently be justified by even modest gains in alignment or problem-solving.

## Rising Productivity Changes the Math

The speaker contrasts the earlier $250,000 per-person output level with a much higher level of $2 million per person. At that higher productivity, the implied value of each hour of focused work is dramatically greater. Under those conditions, many of the same meetings that used to be acceptable become net negative.

The claim is that as individual productivity rises, the cost of pulling people into a meeting grows faster than organizations realize. What once was a reasonable way to coordinate becomes value-destroying, because the time spent coordinating displaces increasingly valuable solo output. The destruction of value is said to scale with how productive people are; the more productive the workforce, the more expensive each unnecessary meeting becomes.

## Misunderstandings About AI and Team Output

The speaker connects this to AI by noting that conversations about AI and teams are dominated by talk of volume. Examples given include more code, more content, and faster output, all emphasizing quantity. This narrow focus on volume ignores the structural implications of higher per-person productivity.

The argument is that when organizations pursue “more, faster” without reexamining how they coordinate, they make organizational decisions that are wrong at a fundamental level. Coordination practices, especially meetings, are not being recalibrated to match the new opportunity costs created by AI-boosted productivity. As a result, companies can adopt AI, increase per-person output capacity, yet inadvertently destroy value by clinging to legacy meeting habits whose costs now exceed their benefits.

## Consequences for Organizational Decisions

By obsessing over volume alone, leaders risk designing workflows and team structures as if coordination costs had not changed. The speaker characterizes the resulting decisions as disastrously incorrect, implying a substantial gap between perceived and actual value creation. The underlying message is that organizations must reconsider not just how much work AI lets them do, but also how they coordinate that work in a high-output environment.
