## Key Points

- Modern societies place an unprecedentedly high value on IQ-like intelligence and years of schooling.
- Historical religious and literary texts emphasize traits like strength, courage, religiosity, or beauty rather than abstract intelligence.
- IQ-test performance may not align with the kind of practical problem-solving needed in small-band subsistence environments.
- Genetic variants influencing IQ, obesity, walking pace, schooling, and wealth are substantially shared and correlated.
- In 20th-century Iceland, there is evidence of selection against a variant combination associated with higher education and wealth.
- Reich frames a trade-off between having many children with low investment and few children with high investment.
- He suggests schizophrenia and bipolar disorder may be extreme outcomes of trait spectrums that can be advantageous in some cultural roles, like shamanistic or visionary functions.
- Complex traits often remain balanced because different positions on their spectrums bring advantages in different environments, preventing one-sided selection.

## Notes

## Modern Valuation of Intelligence

Reich begins by questioning the assumption that intelligence, especially as measured by IQ tests and years of schooling, is universally and timelessly valued. He notes that our current society intensely prizes this narrow form of cognitive ability, tying it to long formal education and high-status careers. He emphasizes that this emphasis is historically unusual.

Looking at older cultural sources, such as the Hebrew and Christian Bible, he points out that intelligence in the IQ-like sense is barely valued at all. The Old Testament era, he notes, appears to coincide with a time when selection for intelligence may have been at a historical peak, yet the texts focus on other virtues. In these texts, the valued traits are strength, courage, religiosity, and similar qualities.

Reich extends this observation to Homer and other religious or classical traditions, where beauty, bravery, or piety are highlighted far more than abstract cognitive prowess. This contrast suggests that our modern “hyperfocus” on being smart and staying in school for many years is a culturally contingent value system, not a universal human standard.

## IQ Versus Practical Intelligence in Ancestral Contexts

Reich distinguishes between IQ-test intelligence and broader, practical intelligence that would have mattered in ancestral environments. He raises the possibility that typical IQ measures are not strongly correlated with the ability to handle new, concrete environmental challenges, such as entering an unfamiliar landscape and successfully securing food and shelter.

He invokes work like Joseph Henrich’s to support the idea that modern people often underestimate how hard it is to live effectively in small bands in demanding environments. Skills such as resource extraction, tool use, navigation, and social coordination in such settings may rely on cognitive capacities that IQ tests only partially tap, if at all.

From this, he suggests one reason we may not see simple, strong selection signals on IQ-test-type intelligence: the trait measured might not map neatly onto the actual adaptive problem-solving capacities that were historically crucial.

## Correlated Complex Traits and Shared Genetic Variance

Reich introduces an important observation from genetics: a wide range of seemingly disparate traits show substantial shared genetic underpinnings. He lists obesity, years of schooling, walking pace, IQ-test performance, and household wealth as examples.

These traits, though superficially different, appear to be partly governed by overlapping combinations of genetic variants. This overlap implies that when selection acts on one trait, it may indirectly shift others because they are genetically correlated. In other words, selection on “intelligence” may also be selection on multiple other life-history and behavioral traits.

He is cautious and speculative but uses this pattern as a basis for thinking about trade-offs: an individual’s genome may push them toward a particular package of outcomes, rather than independently optimizing each trait.

## Icelandic Example and Fertility Trade-Offs

Reich discusses Iceland over the last century as a case where selection seems to have acted against this shared variant combination associated with traits like higher schooling and wealth. He proposes a life-history interpretation: two different strategies of parental investment and fertility.

One strategy is to have many children and invest relatively little in each, including less effort in delaying reproduction to acquire schooling or wealth. The other strategy is to defer childbearing, accumulate resources, pursue education, and invest heavily in fewer children.

In a context of material abundance, such as 20th-century Iceland, having many children with relatively lower investment per child may lead to higher total reproductive success. This would generate selection against the variant combination correlated with higher education and wealth, because those individuals tend to have fewer children.

He likens this to classic ecological contrasts: mammals generally produce few offspring with high parental investment, while many fish release large numbers of offspring with minimal investment, most of which die. Different environments favor different strategies. He suggests that human populations may toggle between these strategies across times and places, leading to oscillating selection on the shared variant set linked to intelligence, health, and wealth.

## Mental Illness, Creativity, and Context-Dependent Advantage

Reich then turns to schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, asking how such debilitating conditions could persist if they are purely harmful. He suggests that what we observe clinically as schizophrenia or bipolar disorder might be extreme manifestations of broader trait spectrums.

Along these spectrums, moderate levels of related traits—such as heightened anxiety, strong imagination, or neuroticism—might be advantageous in certain cultural or ecological contexts. He mentions shamanistic or religious traditions as examples, where individuals capable of visions or unusual experiences may be highly valued.

In such societies, a person with subclinical, milder versions of traits related to schizophrenia or bipolar disorder might play important roles as visionaries or creative figures. Thus, selection might preserve genetic variants that increase risk of these disorders because they also promote beneficial traits in many carriers.

This reflects the same general idea: creativity, unusual thinking, or emotional sensitivity may be adaptive in some niches, even if their extremes manifest as mental illness.

## Balancing Selection on Complex Traits

Reich concludes with a general claim about complex traits: they are not simply pushed in a single direction by natural selection. Instead, they often lie on spectrums where both ends can have advantages depending on context.

He emphasizes that the traits he discusses—intelligence, educational attainment, obesity-related traits, mental illness risk, and others—have multidimensional impacts. Different environments, cultures, and value systems can reward different combinations and degrees of these traits.

As a result, selection pressures can shift over time and place, favoring higher or lower fertility, more or less schooling, more or less risk of certain mental traits, and different types of cognitive style. This ongoing tension helps explain why we do not see simple, uniform evolutionary trends toward ever-higher IQ or the elimination of traits linked to disorders like schizophrenia. Instead, human variation in these domains persists because selection often favors diversity along these complex spectrums.
