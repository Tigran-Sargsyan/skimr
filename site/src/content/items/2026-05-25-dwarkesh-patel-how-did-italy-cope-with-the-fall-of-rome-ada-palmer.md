## Key Points

- Loss of Roman central administration forced individual European cities to manage governance, security, and supply routes themselves.
- Larger, wealthier towns with good surrounding farmland could sustain themselves and adopt republican institutions led by elite councils.
- Weaker towns that lacked resources or population were vulnerable to domination by a single powerful family creating a local monarchy.
- Many fearful urban residents left insecure towns to live near fortified rural villas, forming village communities under a lord’s protection.
- These villa-centered villages were effectively monarchic structures based on dependence on a local noble’s protection.
- Italy’s particularly strong agriculture allowed more of its cities to remain self-sustaining republics than in many other regions.
- Italian city-republics remained militarily small and could not field armies large enough to resist serious mercenary forces.
- Over time, Italian republics were repeatedly converted into monarchies by ambitious families or outsiders, prompting elaborate institutional checks to resist such takeovers.

## Notes

## Collapse of Roman Central Authority

- With the dissolution of the Western Roman Empire, cities could no longer rely on a centralized imperial government.
- Essential services once coordinated by Rome—securing roads from bandits, organizing large-scale trade routes, and maintaining infrastructure—disappeared.
- Cities suddenly had to handle their own security, logistics, and governance without imperial oversight or resources.

## Diverging Paths: Strong Towns vs. Weak Towns

- Larger, wealthier towns with good agricultural land and attached farms could support themselves from nearby resources.
- These towns were better positioned to transition into self-governing entities modeled on Roman precedents.
- In such places, leading families formed councils or senates, reviving a republican style of government where local elites collectively ruled.
- Poorer or smaller towns, lacking sufficient people or food, struggled to self-sustain and were much more vulnerable.
- In these weaker towns, a single wealthy family could hire armed retainers (“goons”), dominate rivals, and declare themselves monarchs.

## Flight to the Villas and Birth of Villages

- In insecure, under-resourced towns, inhabitants feared both starvation and robbery by desperate people.
- Outside these towns, wealthy landowners’ villas offered fortified residences and private bodyguards.
- Townspeople sought safety by moving near such villas, offering labor in exchange for protection.
- This migration emptied many towns and created “villages” in the literal sense: a villa and its immediate environs.
- These villages were monarchic in structure, centered on the authority of a local lordling who controlled protection and resources.
- Over time, some of these villages grew into larger settlements, even cities, but still rooted in that protective, lord-centered origin.

## Why Italy Produced More Republics

- Italy’s strong agricultural base and fertile land meant more cities could reliably feed themselves from nearby countryside.
- Because of this, a larger proportion of Italian towns remained viable as urban centers instead of dissolving into villa-dominated villages.
- Consequently, more Italian cities preserved or developed republican forms of government compared with many other European regions.

## The Long-Term Challenge: Staying a Republic

- Establishing a republic around 500 AD did not guarantee its survival over the next millennium.
- Italian cities were often very large and wealthy by medieval and Renaissance standards, sometimes housing 100,000–150,000 people.
- Yet even these populations were too small to support large standing armies comparable to professional mercenary forces.
- A professional military entrepreneur or ambitious noble with mercenaries could overpower a city’s citizen militia.
- This military weakness made city-republics structurally vulnerable to monarchic takeover.

## Internal and External Threats of Takeover

- Threats came from both insiders and outsiders.
- Within a city, one powerful family might try to eliminate or dominate rival families and seize sole control.
- The rivalry in Romeo and Juliet is used as an illustrative model: families like the Montagues and Capulets fight until one could, in principle, wipe out the other and rule the city.
- Alternatively, a defeated elite family expelled from one city might relocate and capture a neighboring city if that city’s elites were too weak to resist their hired muscle.
- Outsiders might similarly see a rich but militarily weak republic as an attractive target for establishing a duchy or similar monarchy.

## Institutional Responses and Constant Anxiety

- Italian city-republics lived in continual fear of being converted into monarchies by powerful families or foreign-backed rulers.
- In response, they invested great effort in crafting complex political institutions and balances of power.
- These institutions aimed specifically to prevent any one family or strongman from accumulating enough power to overthrow the republic.
- The broader pattern is an ongoing tension: urban wealth and republican ideals versus the ever-present risk of militarized, personal rule.
