## Key Points

- Deploying AI at scale requires building institutional trust through guardrails, audit trails, and human oversight.
- The time needed for oversight and governance cannot be shortened by faster benchmark improvements.
- AI will transform the economy, but not on the accelerated timeline implied by current market expectations.
- Apocalyptic labor displacement scenarios assume a speed of change that social inertia will not allow.
- Exuberant growth scenarios assume adoption and integration speeds that organizational realities will not allow.
- Real-world AI progress is slower, messier, and more unevenly distributed than either doom or boom narratives suggest.
- AI capability growth follows a steep, fast-rising curve across reasoning, intelligence, and agentic performance.
- Societal dissipation of AI capabilities into workflows, money flows, and institutions is a much flatter, slower curve governed by inertia forces.

## Notes

## Capability Growth vs. Real-World Adoption

The speaker distinguishes between two different curves: AI capability and societal dissipation. AI capability represents how fast models improve on intelligence, reasoning depth, and agentic behavior. This curve is described as very steep, with rapid gains clearly visible in benchmarks and product updates. As an example, Gemini is said to have doubled its reasoning ability in just three months, illustrating how quickly the technical side is advancing.

Societal dissipation is the second, often ignored, curve. It represents how quickly these technical capabilities actually spread through the economy. This includes changes in how work is done, how money moves, and how institutions function. Unlike the capability curve, the societal dissipation curve is much flatter, starting from a low base and compounding only gradually over time.

## Role of Human Oversight and Governance

To deploy AI at scale, institutions must build trust around its use. This involves demonstrating appropriate guardrails, robust audit trails, and meaningful human oversight. These governance structures reassure stakeholders that AI is being used responsibly. However, putting them in place takes substantial time.

The speaker emphasizes that this time requirement is not something that can be reduced just by making the models better on benchmarks. Even dramatic capability improvements do not compress the organizational and regulatory processes required. Human oversight and governance thus act as drag forces on deployment speed, but they are framed as necessary elements for safe, scalable integration rather than disposable obstacles.

## Why Doom and Boom Timelines Are Both Wrong

The speaker addresses two opposing narratives: doomers and boomers. Doomers expect rapid, large-scale labor displacement and associated societal disruption. This view presumes that AI can reshape the labor market at a speed that social inertia will not permit. In other words, social systems, norms, and resistance slow down how quickly workers can be replaced or roles radically changed.

On the other side, boomers expect extraordinarily fast adoption, productivity gains, and economic transformation driven by AI. This requires organizations to integrate AI at a speed that is unrealistic given internal processes, legacy systems, compliance requirements, and human change management. Organizational reality becomes a brake on the optimistic growth story.

The speaker argues that both extremes misjudge the rate at which AI permeates actual institutions. The doom narrative overestimates how quickly society will accept massive disruption. The boom narrative overestimates how quickly organizations can successfully integrate complex new tools.

## The Gap Where We Live

The gap between the fast capability curve and the slow societal dissipation curve is described as the space where everyone currently lives. This gap explains much of the apparent confusion and tension around AI today. On one hand, there are visible, dramatic capability milestones that suggest an imminent revolution. On the other hand, everyday work and institutional practices change only incrementally.

Because this gap is large and growing, people experience a mismatch between hype and lived reality. Markets may price in rapid transformation based on capability curves, while workplaces lag. Narratives of sudden catastrophe or instant abundance both overlook the friction encoded in this gap.

Over time, the slower curve does compound, meaning that AI will have significant effects. But the path is slower, messier, and more uneven geographically, sectorally, and institutionally than either the doom or boom stories allow. This framework of two curves, and the gap between them, is presented as the key to understanding the current AI moment and reconciling rapid technical progress with gradual societal change.
