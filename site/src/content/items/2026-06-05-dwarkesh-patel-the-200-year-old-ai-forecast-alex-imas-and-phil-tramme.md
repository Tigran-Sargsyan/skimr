## Key Points

- David Ricardo correctly anticipated that many industrial-era jobs would be automated away by machines.
- Despite widespread automation of Ricardo-era jobs, prime-age employment today is near historical highs.
- Ricardo failed to foresee structural change in which cheaper goods freed income for spending on new services.
- This historical episode undercuts the lump of labor intuition that job opportunities are a fixed quantity.
- Imas refuses to infer from history that AI will necessarily preserve full employment or labor share.
- He suggests economists write models for divergent futures, such as labor share falling to zero or remaining stable.
- Current economic data on consumer demand elasticities and task-level job changes are sparse and low quality.
- Imas calls for a “Manhattan Project for data” to track job creation, destruction, and key scarcity dimensions.

## Notes

## Ricardo’s 200-year-old automation worry

- In the early 1800s, classical economist David Ricardo first welcomed the Industrial Revolution, expecting lower prices and broad benefits.
- He then reversed course after noticing that many existing, value-creating jobs appeared on track to be automated by machines.
- Ricardo predicted that this automation would be harmful: mass unemployment, political unrest, and social instability.
- If we look backward, he was right in a narrow sense: most of the jobs that were lucrative in his era really did get automated over time.

## Why his prediction still failed on employment

- Imas imagines asking a resurrected Ricardo what he thinks prime-age employment would be in 2026, after learning all his era’s jobs were automated.
- Ricardo would likely expect a very low employment rate, consistent with his pessimistic forecast.
- Instead, prime-age employment is near its historical maximum, second only to around the year 2000.
- So while Ricardo correctly foresaw job automation, he missed the aggregate outcome: high overall employment persisted.

## Structural change and the lump of labor fallacy

- Imas attributes this to “economics of structural change”: when automated sectors become cheap, consumers have more disposable income.
- That extra income flows somewhere, and historically it flowed heavily into services, which created new types of jobs.
- This dynamic contradicts the “lump of labor” intuition that there is a fixed amount of work to be done.
- Ricardo did not fully account for the possibility that automation-induced savings would shift demand into entirely new sectors.
- Imas stresses that it was not obvious ex ante that demand would go to services rather than even more automated goods; this path was contingent, not guaranteed.

## Lessons for forecasting AI’s impact on labor

- Imas explicitly rejects using Ricardo’s story as a simple reassurance that AI will also preserve full employment.
- Instead, he uses the story to highlight how hard it is to make long-run predictions about labor markets.
- The same kind of reasoning error—focusing on visible disappearing jobs without anticipating structural shifts—could mislead us today in either optimistic or pessimistic directions.

## Modeling divergent scenarios for labor share

- Imas proposes that economists start with simple, extreme premises and build models around them.
- One model might assume labor’s share of income has fallen to zero and ask what mechanisms could generate that outcome.
- Another model might assume labor share remains stable and explore what economic forces would sustain it.
- Phil Trammell is mentioned as someone who will discuss such modeling later in the broader conversation.
- The goal is not to pick a single forecast but to map a range of logically coherent scenarios.

## The data gap and a “Manhattan Project for data”

- Imas’s central message is that we lack the data needed to adjudicate between these scenarios.
- He emphasizes that we do not have good estimates of consumer demand elasticities, which are crucial for understanding how spending shifts when prices fall.
- We also are not systematically tracking which specific jobs and tasks are being created or destroyed in enough detail.
- The O*NET database, which catalogues job tasks, is rarely updated and considered by him to be low quality for this purpose.
- Because of these gaps, he argues for a “Manhattan Project for data” to build high-quality, timely data on tasks, occupations, and spending responses.

## Using scenarios to guide data collection

- Imas suggests that carefully articulated scenarios should inform which data we prioritize collecting.
- For a future with full employment, we might focus on sectors where human relationships and non-automatable services could remain scarce.
- For a future where labor’s share collapses, we would investigate different scarcity dimensions and mechanisms driving capital dominance.
- By linking each scenario to the specific “dimension of scarcity” it relies on, economists can identify the exact measurements needed.
- This approach turns uncertainty about AI’s impact into a structured research agenda rather than a single fragile forecast.
