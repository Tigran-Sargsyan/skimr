## Key Points

- The internet is moving from an attention economy to an AI‑mediated interpretation economy.
- AI agents increasingly act as the first point of contact for purchase and hiring decisions.
- Marketers must create a structured, factual “truth layer” about products that agents can read and trust.
- Without clear, opinionated product data, AI flattens brands into undifferentiated category averages.
- Individuals need a personal truth layer that proves real AI‑relevant skills, not just polished profiles.
- Back‑office AI automation for content or resumes is now table stakes rather than a differentiator.
- Brand loyalty and human memory still matter and become more valuable as more transactions are mediated by AI.
- The strongest strategies make companies both highly legible to agents and deeply memorable to humans.

## Notes

## From Attention Economy to Interpretation Economy
- For roughly 25 years, the internet economy has been built around capturing human attention: ads, clicks, funnels, and brand visibility.
- With AI, a fundamental shift is underway: the central bottleneck is no longer grabbing attention but being correctly interpreted by AI systems.
- Instead of asking “How do I get people to notice me or my product?”, the key question becomes “How will an AI answer when someone asks whether to trust me or buy from me?”
- This applies both to companies and to individuals seeking jobs; the same structural change affects marketing funnels and hiring funnels.

## Concrete Example: Buying a Sound System Through AI
- The speaker recently bought a sound system entirely by conversing with AI models (Claude and ChatGPT).
- He provided specifics like room dimensions, budget, and sound preferences (warm vs cool) and received tailored recommendations.
- He cannot know if he got the objectively best product, but he accepted what the AI presented as best, which is what matters commercially.
- The marketers for the sound systems he chose likely had very little direct influence; AI mediation determined the consideration set.
- That gap—between the truly best option and what the AI presents as best—is economically massive and will determine who gets discovered.

## The “Prove‑It” Interpretation Economy
- In the interpretation economy, AI agents filter the web and map user intent to products and people.
- Being in the AI’s consideration set is the new core marketing and career problem.
- This requires shifting effort from purely attention‑seeking behaviors toward making yourself or your product machine‑interpretable and provably differentiated.

## Back‑Office Automation is Table Stakes
- Many marketers focus on automating internal tasks with AI: drafting copy, reports, or content faster.
- Individuals similarly use AI to customize resumes, cover letters, and job searches.
- These uses are important but are “table stakes” by 2026, not sources of lasting advantage.
- The real leverage lies in positioning products and people for an AI‑first world, not just using AI behind the scenes.

## The Truth Layer for Companies
- Marketers need a “truth layer”: accurate, high‑fidelity, structured data about products that can be reliably consumed by agents.
- AI agents (including those inside tools like ChatGPT) send out search agents to gather and interpret web data, whether users realize it or not.
- The truth layer must bridge what customers say and mean with how products are described in machine‑readable form.
- If a company is not clearly opinionated—unable to articulate why its product is uniquely good and to prove it—AI will flatten it into “internet average” for its category.

## From Emotional Copy to Provable Claims
- Traditional marketing relies heavily on emotional, human‑oriented language and broad claims.
- In the prove‑it economy, agents need factual, structured, evidence‑backed descriptions instead of vague superlatives.
- Example: a running shoe brand must detail its unique spring system, materials, and measurable impact on energy return or joint impact.
- Those claims must be backed by data and exposed in formats AI can parse—clear web content, extractable DOM, or structured data like JSON schemas.
- Without this, agents cannot correctly map a runner’s intent (e.g., reduce knee impact) to that brand’s shoe, so the brand never appears as a strong recommendation.

## Marketers Must Embrace Technical Surfaces
- Many marketers dismiss such work as “too technical” or outside their job description.
- However, the leverage in modern marketing is precisely in shaping these technical surfaces so agents can understand and favor the product.
- Marketing must care about how product information is structured, tagged, and exposed on the open web, not just how it looks to humans.

## Parallel Challenge for Individuals: Personal Truth Layers
- Individuals are subject to the same dynamics as products, especially in the AI‑driven hiring market.
- The speaker’s “talent board” project is framed around proving AI‑relevant skills for candidates.
- Merely claiming skills or polishing LinkedIn is not enough; candidates must demonstrate capabilities like building MLOps pipelines, agent development pipelines, or shipping production‑quality work via AI.
- Proof could be based on real projects and artifacts that an AI (and human) can examine, forming a personal truth layer.
- When an AI is asked about a candidate, this layer provides detailed, verifiable evidence rather than generic buzzwords.

## Data Availability Internally and Externally
- Organizations already understand that good internal data is essential for internal AI agents to work effectively.
- The same principle must be extended externally: product truth data must be on the broader internet for public agents to interpret.
- Similarly, individuals need externalized evidence—public or shareable data—that demonstrates what they can do.
- Without such data, both products and people will be misinterpreted or ignored by AI‑driven selection processes.

## Two Ways People Will Buy in an AI World
- There are two main patterns for purchasing and hiring in an AI‑rich world:
  1. Ask an AI to interpret options and transact on the user’s behalf.
  2. Ask by brand or person name because strong loyalty and memory already exist.
- For the first path, agent legibility and strong truth layers dominate.
- For the second, offline and emotional brand building dominate; the AI becomes a constrained executor (“buy from this brand”), not a broad comparator.

## Brand Loyalty and Human Memory Become More Valuable
- The speaker argues that brand and emotion do not become less important; they become more scarce and thus more valuable.
- As more of the transaction is mediated by agents, the amount of human attention and memory shrinks, making each instance more precious.
- If a brand had built an emotional bond with him, he would have gone straight to that brand rather than asking AI for a wide search.
- Human preference still chooses among AI‑presented options, and prior brand loyalty can pre‑commit a user to a particular choice.

## Two Internets: Human‑Facing and Agent‑Facing
- There is a “two internet” reality:
  - Human‑facing work: memory, preference, emotional trust, and language.
  - Agent‑facing work: clarity, structure, evidence, and retrievability.
- If a company’s human brand says one thing but the machine‑readable truth layer says something else or is incoherent, both sides weaken.
- A memorable story without a coherent product truth layer gets flattened by agents.
- A well‑structured truth layer without memorable human branding gets surfaced in comparisons but fails to be chosen by humans.
- The best marketers will intentionally serve both internets rather than choosing one.

## Dangers of AI‑Washing
- Companies and individuals feel pressure to look “AI‑native” because AI‑associated roles and products command higher value.
- AI‑washing—overstating or fabricating AI capabilities—risks misleading customers and employers.
- This leads to disappointment, broken trust, and ultimately weaker positioning when the truth emerges.
- Instead, the advice is to build on genuine expertise and curiosity, and clearly state what can actually be done.
- That same honest, specific differentiation is what agents need to treat products and people as distinct rather than average.

## Evaluating Marketing Roles and Company Readiness
- When choosing a role or assessing a company, one key test is how much influence marketing has over critical surfaces.
- Important questions include: Does marketing meaningfully shape the website, pricing clarity, product claims, launch process, sales collateral, and support explanations?
- If marketing is merely a “content factory” decorating decisions made elsewhere, it is a weak role in an AI era.
- If a company says it wants “AI marketing” but only means producing more assets with fewer people, its strategy is shallow.
- Strong roles and companies let marketing increase agent legibility, human memorability, and internal operational clarity by being close to product, sales, and technical teams.

## Becoming Technical Enough Without Being an Engineer
- Marketing is becoming more technical, but not to the point that it should be run solely by engineers.
- Non‑engineers who are willing to become “a little bit technical” and understand how agents work will have an advantage.
- Toolkits and frameworks (like the speaker’s Substack materials) are aimed at helping marketers and candidates build truth layers without deep engineering skills.

## Rethinking Personal and Product Positioning
- Traditional job‑seeker advice—“market yourself,” post on LinkedIn, get attention—is grounded in the legacy attention economy.
- Now, individuals must allocate effort into two deliberate buckets:
  1. Making themselves deeply memorable to humans, often via offline interactions and relationships.
  2. Making themselves agent‑legible through detailed, differentiated, data‑rich descriptions of what they can do.
- Products must follow the same pattern: cultivate offline and online brand experiences while investing in a robust, structured truth layer.

## The Importance of Opinions and Differentiation
- In an AI‑mediated landscape, trying to be “for everyone” and avoiding strong opinions leads to being averaged out.
- Agents look for clear differentiation; if they cannot see what makes a person or product distinct, they compress it toward the mean.
- Many people use AI tools to generate generic, polished language, which increases noise and decreases signal.
- To stand out, individuals and brands must articulate specific, concrete, sometimes “spicy” opinions about their strengths and focus.

## Strategic Takeaway
- Do not equate AI adoption with mere back‑office automation of content or applications; that is baseline.
- The core strategic work is to anticipate how AI agents interpret the web and orient both your personal career and your company’s marketing around that.
- Success will come from being both agent‑readable and human‑memorable, backed by honest, provable differentiation rather than generic AI‑washed claims.
