## Key Points

- The Bronze Age covers about 3,000 years, allowing compound evolutionary changes in human genetics.
- Pigmentation-related genetic variants show their strongest selection signal 2,000–4,000 years ago.
- Genetic variants associated with modern cognitive performance measures also show strong selection in that same 2,000–4,000 year window.
- Polygenic scores predicting IQ test performance, years of schooling, and household wealth today shifted by about one standard deviation over 10,000 years.
- Selection on these cognitive-related polygenic scores peaks in the Bronze Age and is roughly two standard deviations over that period.
- There is essentially no detectable natural selection on these cognitive-related scores in the last 2,000 years after accounting for ancestry.
- The method uses rolling 2,000-year windows to estimate selection strength across time from ancient DNA.
- Ali Akbari’s methodology corrects for changing ancestry by analyzing many small, relatively homogeneous populations across space and time and looking for consistent directional genetic changes within them.

## Notes

## Timeframe and Compound Effects
- The Bronze Age is characterized as lasting about 3,000 years, not just a few centuries.
- This long duration allows “compound interest”–like evolutionary effects, where small, continuous selection pressures accumulate into large genetic shifts.
- The speaker emphasizes that this period appears unusually impactful for human evolution when looking at complex traits.

## Selection on Pigmentation
- Pigmentation provides the strongest signal of selection among the complex traits examined in their dataset.
- Researchers aggregate the effects of dozens or hundreds of known pigmentation-affecting mutations into a polygenic score across the genome.
- When they track how this score changes over time, the period of strongest natural selection on pigmentation is between about 2,000 and 4,000 years ago.
- This implies a particularly intense evolutionary reshaping of pigmentation traits in the later prehistoric to early historic period.

## Cognitive-Related Traits and Modern Polygenic Scores
- They analyze genetic variants associated with modern measures of cognitive performance, such as intelligence test scores in white British individuals today.
- These variants also form polygenic predictors that correlate with years of schooling and household wealth in present-day populations.
- The speaker notes these are “crazy traits in the past” because there were no intelligence tests, formal schooling, or modern wealth structures thousands of years ago.
- Despite that, the same sets of variants can be tracked back in time to see how their frequencies changed.
- Over roughly 10,000 years, the polygenic score for these cognitive-related traits shifts by about one standard deviation on the scale of current variation, indicating a substantial overall evolutionary change.

## Temporal Pattern of Selection on Cognitive-Related Traits
- To localize when selection was strongest, they slide a 2,000-year window across the 18,000-year dataset and re-estimate selection within each window.
- For the cognitive-related polygenic scores, the selection strength peaks between about 5,000 and 2,000 years ago, i.e., broadly within the Bronze Age timeframe.
- During this Bronze Age peak, the average strength of selection corresponds to about two standard deviations of movement in the polygenic score over that interval, referenced to the 10,000-year scale.
- In contrast, in the last 2,000 years there is almost no detectable signal of natural selection on these cognitive-related polygenic predictors.
- This result contradicts the intuitive expectation that industrialization or more recent historical complexity might have intensified selection on such traits.

## Methodological Approach and Ancestry Correction
- The ancient DNA dataset is heterogeneous, including Southern Europeans, Northern Europeans, hunter-gatherers, and various farmer groups that change in representation over time.
- Simple temporal comparisons would be confounded by large-scale migrations and ancestry shifts.
- Ali Akbari’s methodology addresses this by correcting for changing ancestry when estimating selection.
- The dataset is conceptually divided into an “archipelago” of small populations: discrete pockets defined by specific regions and time slices (e.g., Britain 4,000–3,500 years ago, Hungary in a given interval, Italy 2,000–1,500 years ago).
- Within each small population, ancestry is relatively stable over the short time window, reducing disruption by migration.
- Researchers then examine whether, across these many pockets, the genetic changes in trait-associated variants consistently “blow” in the same direction.
- By aggregating these within-population directional changes, they infer the strength of natural selection at different times, after correcting for major population turnover events.

## Overall Implications in This Clip
- Multiple complex traits, especially pigmentation and cognitive-related polygenic scores tied to modern measures of intelligence, education, and wealth, show their strongest selection signals in the Bronze Age.
- Evolutionary change on these traits appears to have been far more intense 2,000–4,000 years ago than in the most recent two millennia.
- The findings rely on polygenic analyses across ancient genomes, carefully adjusted for shifting ancestry through a fine-grained, population-specific analytical framework.
