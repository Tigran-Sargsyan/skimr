## Notes

## Outdated Educational Philosophy

The speaker claims that around 2 billion children are enrolled in schools built on an educational philosophy suited to a 20th‑century industrial economy. This philosophy assumes a world of stable jobs, standardized tasks, and predictable career paths. He stresses that this industrial economy will not exist by the time today’s students reach adulthood. The implication is that the knowledge and skills schools are optimizing for are misaligned with the future these children will face.

He underscores that this is not an abstract policy issue but a personal one, noting that he has children himself. This personal stake frames the concern as urgent and immediate, not theoretical.

## Claim that AGI Has Arrived

To illustrate how dramatically the world may be shifting, he cites a peer‑reviewed argument published in the scientific journal Nature. This article contends that artificial general intelligence has indeed arrived. He quotes the article’s assertion that “The machines Turing envisioned 75 years ago have arrived.”

By invoking Turing’s original concept of machines with human‑level cognitive abilities, the speaker positions current AI as potentially crossing a threshold from narrow tools to broadly capable systems. This claim, presented as coming from a respected journal, is used to emphasize that we may already be in an era that traditional schooling never anticipated.

## Rapid Growth of AI Use in Learning

The speaker notes that AI is not just a theoretical capability; students are already using it at scale. He cites the Digital Education Council, saying 86% of students globally report using AI in their learning. This suggests AI tools have become mainstream in educational practice, regardless of whether formal curricula have caught up.

He then focuses on the UK as a concrete example of accelerating adoption. According to HEPI’s annual student survey, AI usage among students in the UK jumped from 66% in 2024 to 92% in 2025. He emphasizes the speed of this change, describing the picture as “even more dramatic” in the UK. The specific numbers convey that within a single year, AI moved from common to nearly universal among students.

The central point he draws from these statistics is growth: adoption is not static or incremental but rapidly rising, indicating a structural shift in how learners access help and information.

## Performance of AI Tutors

Beyond adoption rates, he highlights evidence about effectiveness. He asserts that AI tutors outperform human tutors in controlled studies. This indicates that AI is not merely more accessible; it may also provide superior educational outcomes in certain contexts.

By framing this result in terms of controlled studies, he signals that the claim rests on experimental comparison rather than anecdote. If AI tutors are demonstrably better on key learning measures, then the traditional assumption that human tutoring is the gold standard is challenged.

## Institutional and Parental Uncertainty

Despite the arrival of powerful AI, widespread student use, and evidence of AI tutors’ superior performance, he argues that major stakeholders remain unprepared. He states that schools, governments, and parents have not yet figured out what to teach children in this new environment.

The core unresolved question is what comes next for education in a world changing this fast. Existing curricula and policies do not yet reflect a coherent answer about the roles of AI, the skills humans should prioritize, or how to structure learning when intelligent systems are ubiquitous. The tension he highlights is between rapidly advancing capabilities and slowly adapting educational systems, leaving children caught in a mismatch between their schooling and their future reality.
