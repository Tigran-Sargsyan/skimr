## Key Points

- AI means polished memos, plans, and prototypes no longer reliably signal actual understanding or judgment.
- The core scarce skill now is judgment and sensemaking, not generating more or prettier artifacts.
- Live whiteboard-style conversations expose how someone notices, reasons, updates, and decides under pressure.
- A strong whiteboard session makes four elements explicit: situation, decision, risk, and change.
- Rejected options reveal what someone understood and consciously chose not to do, which is crucial evidence.
- Surfacing risks, including prevented failures, turns invisible protective work into visible proof of judgment.
- Portfolios still matter, but they must include evidence of reasoning, not just finished outputs.
- Nate’s Talent Board concept aims to store and showcase this reasoning evidence as a career asset.

## Notes

## AI Has Broken the Old Signals of “Being Good at Work”

Microsoft reports that 86% of people treat AI output as a starting point, not the final answer, and 58% are producing work they could not have produced a year ago, rising above 80% among advanced users. This confirms that AI dramatically boosts visible productivity and polish. But because AI raises the floor of how good artifacts can look, traditional signals like sharp resumes, clean strategy docs, and functioning prototypes no longer reliably show whether someone truly understands their work. A memo can read great and a plan can look organized even if the underlying judgment is weak.

The core issue is not resumes themselves but the kind of evidence we use to judge competence. In a pre-AI world, producing a roadmap or strategy document already required significant expertise, so the artifact carried a lot of signal about the person behind it. AI breaks this link by making “looking productive” easy. The scarce value now is not generation but comprehension and sensemaking: what we question, what we keep, what we reject, and how we decide.

## The Need for Visible Human Judgment

In the AI age, we need better ways to see human judgment at work. The important things to reveal are what someone noticed, what they believed, what they rejected, which risks they saw, what changed because they were involved, and how their thinking holds up when challenged by another serious person. Work samples and portfolios, if they only show polished artifacts, are downstream of this real thinking and miss the crucial evidence.

The difficulty is that it is now very hard to see past the “shiny portfolio” into the underlying judgment. Standard advice to “build a portfolio” is therefore incomplete. Building and polishing artifacts has become much easier thanks to AI, so portfolios alone are less informative. What hiring managers and collaborators truly need is a window into how someone processes complexity and makes tradeoffs.

## Whiteboarding as the Live Evidence of Judgment

The speaker argues that the “AI age is the age of whiteboards.” To know whether someone really understands a problem, they want to see that person at a whiteboard with someone strong enough to push them. The problem discussed should be real, the room serious. The candidate must draw what they know, name what they do not, identify system fragilities, locate risk, state the choice they would make, and then withstand informed pushback.

A whiteboard conversation is valuable because it converts private judgment into visible human work before the work is cleaned up by AI. In that session, the person has to think in real time, hold the situation in their head, respond to pressure, update when they learn something, and show clearly where their confidence ends. This “live reasoning” is the evidence we now desperately need. It shows not perfect answers but how someone learns, adapts, and maintains useful boundaries under challenge.

## The Four Elements: Situation, Decision, Risk, Change

A strong whiteboard-style conversation should surface four key elements: situation, decision, risk, and change.

### 1. Situation

First, you write down the situation. This includes what is happening, who is involved, how the system works, which constraints matter, which facts are known or missing, where pressure comes from, and why it is hard. Context is where judgment begins. Showing that context demonstrates that you understand the environment, not just isolated tasks.

### 2. Decision

Second, you make the decision explicit. You lay out the plausible paths, state which one you would take, and which you would reject. You clarify where the decision truly sits. Good work involves rejecting plausible options in favor of what really matters, and those rejected options reveal what you understood and refused to handwave away.

### 3. Risk

Third, you surface risk. You ask what could go wrong, which risks you are willing to take, which you are trying to remove, and which you consciously accept because the alternatives are worse. Risk is one of the clearest ways to make invisible work visible, because good judgment often looks like “nothing bad happened.” Naming risks shows that the absence of bad outcomes was not luck but the result of deliberate choices.

Examples include preventing a bad launch, avoiding customer churn, or stopping a flawed model output from reaching production without review. Prevented losses count, but only if you name the risk and your role in handling it.

### 4. Change

Fourth, you specify change. If a decision is made, you describe what becomes different: what gets clearer, safer, or faster; what work stops; what decisions stop being relitigated; and what the team understands afterward that it did not understand before. This keeps the exercise from becoming a diary of everything that happened. The point is to connect your judgment explicitly to a concrete shift in the work.

## Showing How You Learn Under Pressure

Beyond the four elements, a crucial part of the whiteboard experience is how you behave when challenged. Do you get defensive when someone pushes back? Do you update too quickly just to please the room? Or can you hold a useful line when your argument remains sound, while still adjusting where the evidence demands it?

We are not looking for perfect confidence or perfect recall, but for judgment under pressure. The whiteboard becomes a live story about how you process AI-generated content, domain knowledge, and constraints into decisions. It reveals how your thinking sharpens when exposed to another serious mind.

## From Live Session to Lasting Evidence: Talent Board

This need for visible judgment motivated the speaker’s “Nate’s Talent Board” project. Standard career advice to build portfolios underestimates how AI has made generation and polishing easier, thereby diluting the value of portfolios alone. The truly scarce thing is comprehension.

The Talent Board frame emphasizes three ideas: comprehension over generation, explanation as the artifact, and a record of real work instead of mere credentials. A resume can say you are qualified, and a portfolio can show what you made, but the upgraded version says: here is the work and here is the evidence that I understood it, made sense of it, and made good choices. Whiteboarding is the live version of that; Talent Board is where that evidence can live afterward as a persistent record.

In practice, you take a real problem, make your reasoning visible, let others push and refine it, then capture what survived as a Talent Board entry, work sample, promotion note, or hiring packet. The platform simply helps put this reasoning in front of hiring managers.

## Starting a New Role in the Age of AI

Most onboarding advice focuses on listening, learning the organization, meeting stakeholders, and getting quick wins. The speaker thinks this is now incomplete. If judgment is the valuable work, starting strong means forming an early point of view and letting others see how that point of view improves.

A practical move in the first month is to ask for a whiteboard session with someone who deeply understands the domain. You might present your current model of the customer problem, where you think the team is overweighting something, the technical constraint you do not yet understand, and the risk you want to validate. Then you invite pushback. If they correct you, you write it down. If they disagree, you ask what evidence would settle the question. If they expose a missing constraint, you add it.

This is not about proving you arrive fully formed, but about showing that you can learn in public without becoming mushy. Your evolving whiteboard becomes a record of sharpened judgment and improved mental models.

## Applying the Discipline Without a Physical Whiteboard

The same discipline can be used without a literal board. You can do this via a shared document, a digital whiteboard, a Loom video, or an annotated prototype. The exact format matters less than the commitment to make reasoning visible while it still feels alive and dynamic.

By doing this, you naturally surface situation, decision, risk, and change as elements of a story about your judgment in the AI era. In effect, you are telling live stories about AI-generated and human-generated inputs so others can see how you shape them into decisions.

## How to Prove You Are Good at Work Now

To prove you are good at work in this environment, you should not start by making artifacts shinier. Instead, start with a real problem, put your reasoning in front of someone who can challenge it, and then preserve what survived in a format that lets others understand your thinking and the choices you made. That preserved, challenge-tested reasoning is the evidence people now need.

The speaker also mentions having created prompts that can be used with tools like Codex or Claude Code to help extract and structure your thinking so that others can understand it. This aligns with the goal of turning tacit judgment into explicit, communicable evidence. Talent Board is presented as one place where such evidence can be housed, supporting careers in a world where AI has commoditized generation but made human judgment more valuable and harder to see.
